\begin{abstract}
This report presents a look-ahead approximate linearization controller combined with artificial potential fields (APF) for safe waypoint navigation of a DJI RoboMaster EP unicycle-model robot in a bounded $4~\text{m}\times4~\text{m}$ workspace, implemented for the simulator-based ECE~486 submission track. The controller reaches a sequence of desired poses while avoiding a second, independently moving robot, subject to velocity, obstacle-distance, and workspace-boundary safety constraints. The controller is validated through a headless evaluator that imports and drives the exact deployed control function across 10 randomized trials, achieving a 10/10 success rate with zero collisions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Proposed Approach}

\subsection{Problem Setup}
The robot is modeled as a unicycle,
\begin{equation}
\dot{x} = v\cos\theta, \quad \dot{y} = v\sin\theta, \quad \dot{\theta} = \omega,
\label{eq:unicycle}
\end{equation}
where $(x,y)$ is the robot's planar position, $\theta$ its heading, and $(v,\omega)$ the commanded linear and angular velocities. Per the professor-confirmed scope for simulator-based submissions, the task is restricted to navigating to a sequence of known poses while avoiding a second robot in the workspace $x,y \in [-2,2]$~m; arm, gripper, stick, and puck interactions are outside the simulator's model and are not required.

\subsection{Look-Ahead Control Point}
A look-ahead point is defined a distance $l$ ahead of the robot along its heading,
\begin{equation}
p = \begin{bmatrix} x + l\cos\theta \\ y + l\sin\theta \end{bmatrix}, \qquad l = 0.30~\text{m}.
\label{eq:lookahead}
\end{equation}
Its velocity is related to the unicycle inputs by
\begin{equation}
\dot p = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{bmatrix}}_{R(\theta)}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & l\end{bmatrix}}_{L(l)}
\begin{bmatrix}v\\ \omega\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:fwdkin}
\end{equation}
Since $\det\big(R(\theta)L(l)\big) = l$, choosing $l>0$ guarantees the inverse map is well defined, so that once a desired $\dot p$ is designed, the unicycle inputs follow directly:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}v\\ \omega\end{bmatrix} = L^{-1}(l)\,R^{T}(\theta)\,\dot p
\;=\;
\begin{bmatrix} \dot p_x\cos\theta + \dot p_y\sin\theta \\[2pt] \dfrac{-\dot p_x\sin\theta + \dot p_y\cos\theta}{l} \end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:invkin}
\end{equation}
This is the approximate linearization method used to convert a virtual single-integrator velocity command on $p$ into physically realizable unicycle commands.

\subsection{Artificial Potential Fields}
The desired velocity of the look-ahead point, $\dot p = F_{att} + F_{rep}$, is generated by combining an attractive force toward the current waypoint $q_{target}$ and a repulsive force from the tracked obstacle at $q_{obs}$:
\begin{equation}
F_{att} = \zeta\,(q_{target} - p),
\label{eq:fatt}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
F_{rep} = \eta\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{\rho_0}\right)\frac{1}{\rho^2}\nabla\rho, \quad \rho = \|p-q_{obs}\| < \rho_0,
\label{eq:frep}
\end{equation}
An analogous repulsive term is applied against each of the four workspace boundaries, using the robot's distance to each wall in place of $\rho$, so that the same potential-field mechanism that avoids the moving obstacle also keeps $p$ within $x,y\in[-2,2]$~m. APF controllers are known to admit local minima where $F_{att}$ and $F_{rep}$ cancel; the waypoint sequence and obstacle motion used here are arranged so this is not encountered in the reported trials, and the minimum-distance clamp and repulsion-magnitude clamp described below further reduce the practical risk of weak local minima.

\paragraph{Convergence considerations.} To characterize this more precisely than a qualitative discussion of local minima, consider the point dynamics in the region where the target is fixed between waypoint switches and the repulsive term is due to the tracked obstacle alone. Let $e=q_{target}-p$ and treat $\dot p = F_{att}+F_{rep}$ as a single-integrator system in $p$. Then $\dot e = -\dot p = -\zeta e - F_{rep}$, and with the candidate Lyapunov function $V=\tfrac12\|e\|^2$,
\[
\dot V = e^{T}\dot e = -\zeta\|e\|^2 - e^{T}F_{rep}.
\]
When $\rho\ge\rho_0$, $F_{rep}=0$ and $\dot V = -\zeta\|e\|^2<0$ for all $e\neq0$, so $p$ converges exponentially to $q_{target}$ at rate $\zeta$. When $\rho<\rho_0$, $F_{rep}$ is active but bounded by the repulsion clamp, $\|F_{rep}\|\le F_{rep,max}$, so
\[
\dot V \le -\zeta\|e\|^2 + \|e\|\,F_{rep,max} = -\|e\|\big(\zeta\|e\|-F_{rep,max}\big).
\]
Thus $\dot V<0$, giving guaranteed descent toward the target, whenever
\[
\|e\| > r^{*} \triangleq \frac{F_{rep,max}}{\zeta} = \frac{2.0}{1.0} = 2.0\text{~m},
\]
using the values in Table~\ref{tab:params}. The controller is therefore formally guaranteed to make progress toward the current waypoint whenever it is more than $2.0$~m away, regardless of the obstacle's position. Inside this radius, $\dot V$ is no longer sign-definite, so the classical APF local-minimum condition $\zeta e = -F_{rep}$ cannot be excluded analytically; in that region convergence is validated empirically rather than proven, consistent with the 10/10 success rate reported in Section~II. Since the wall-repulsion term uses the same clamp, an identical bound holds near a single active wall; near a workspace corner, where two wall terms and the obstacle term can be simultaneously active, the worst-case bound widens to $r^{*}\le 3F_{rep,max}/\zeta = 6.0$~m, which exceeds the workspace diagonal ($\approx5.66$~m) and therefore does not, by itself, certify convergence in that worst-case configuration. This makes the local-minimum limitation explicit and quantified rather than asserting it has been eliminated.

\subsection{Safety Hardening}
To keep the controller well-behaved near singularities and within actuator limits, the implementation applies:
\begin{itemize}
\item Minimum obstacle/wall distance clamp $\rho_{min}=0.12$~m, preventing division blow-up in \eqref{eq:frep} as $\rho\to0$;
\item Repulsion magnitude clamp to $\pm2.0$;
\item Velocity saturation, $v_{max}=0.8$~m/s, $\omega_{max}=3.0$~rad/s;
\item Exponential command smoothing with $\alpha=0.4$ to suppress spikes between control cycles;
\item An obstacle-staleness timeout: if no obstacle pose update has been received within $0.5$~s, the repulsive term is disabled rather than acting on a stale position; and
\item A shutdown handler that unconditionally commands zero velocity on interrupt, exception, or normal exit.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Software Architecture}
The controller is split into a pure function, \texttt{compute\_control\_from\_state()}, that implements Eqs.~\eqref{eq:lookahead}--\eqref{eq:frep} and the safety hardening above with no ROS dependency, and a ROS~2 node, \texttt{SimulatorPlayerNode}, that wraps it with pose subscriptions, parameter declarations, and telemetry logging. Isolating the control law this way lets the identical function be exercised both on the robot and by the headless evaluator described in Section~II, so the reported metrics are guaranteed to reflect the deployed code rather than a re-implementation. A synthetic VRPN publisher (\texttt{mock\_vrpn.py}) and a \texttt{--use-mock} mode allow the full pipeline to be demonstrated without the simulator or physical robots.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Methodology}
A headless evaluator (\texttt{sim\_evaluator.py}) imports \texttt{compute\_control\_from\_state()} directly from the controller module and integrates the resulting unicycle kinematics forward in time, so every reported number is produced by the exact control law used on the robot. Ten trials were run with randomized initial pose and a moving obstacle on an independent circular trajectory. Each trial is scored for success (goal reached within 0.20~m), collision (minimum $\rho<0.12$~m), time to goal, path length, and minimum/average obstacle distance.

\subsection{Aggregate Results}
All 10 trials reached the goal with zero collisions. Time to goal ranged from 1.20--3.65~s, path length from 0.63--2.53~m, and the minimum distance to the obstacle never fell below 0.46~m across any trial, confirming the repulsive term and clamps kept the robot well clear of the collision threshold even in the closest approach case.

\section{Discussion}
The results confirm that the look-ahead approximate linearization law, combined with clamped APF obstacle and wall avoidance, meets the stated success criterion of reaching target poses while avoiding collisions, across a randomized initial-condition sweep. Because the evaluator drives the identical control function used on the robot, these results are directly traceable to the deployed code rather than to a separate simulation model.

Limitations of the current evaluation are threefold. First, the headless evaluator integrates ideal unicycle kinematics and does not model full simulator dynamics, actuator delay, or ROS~2 communication latency, so real-world timing performance may differ from the reported values. Second, the evaluator drives the controller toward a single fixed goal per trial to isolate the convergence-with-avoidance behavior; the three-waypoint patrol behavior and the associated waypoint-switching state machine used in live operation are validated qualitatively through direct demonstration rather than through the automated metrics in Table~\ref{tab:trials}. Third, as with any APF-based method, local minima remain a theoretical risk; the current waypoint and obstacle configuration avoids this in practice, and the minimum-distance and repulsion clamps reduce, but do not formally eliminate, the risk of weak local minima. Future work could incorporate a global planner or stochastic perturbation of the attractive term to address this more rigorously, along with a multi-waypoint automated evaluator to close the gap noted above.
